## 📖 Passage 1: The Rise of Plant-Based Diets

> Over the past decade, plant-based diets have moved from a niche lifestyle choice to a significant global trend. Once associated mainly with a small number of committed vegetarians and vegans, eating fewer animal products has become increasingly common among mainstream consumers. Sales of plant-based meat alternatives have grown rapidly, and many restaurants now offer dedicated vegetarian or vegan menus.
>
> Several factors have contributed to this shift. Concerns about personal health are often cited as a primary motivation. Studies have linked diets high in fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains to reduced risks of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers. Environmental awareness has also played a major role, as producing meat — especially beef — requires significantly more water, land, and energy than producing plant foods. Animal welfare remains another important consideration for many consumers.
>
> Still, the transition to plant-based eating is not without challenges. Some nutrients, such as vitamin B12 and certain types of iron, are more easily obtained from animal sources. Careful planning is needed to ensure a balanced diet, particularly for children, pregnant women, and athletes. Critics also argue that some plant-based substitutes, although marketed as healthy, contain high levels of salt or processed ingredients.
>
> Whatever their individual reasons, more and more people are choosing to reduce their consumption of meat and dairy. Whether this represents a lasting change in how humans eat, or simply a passing trend, remains to be seen.

## 📖 Passage 2: Why Some Languages Are Dying Out

> Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world today, linguists estimate that nearly half are at risk of disappearing by the end of this century. A language is considered endangered when it is no longer being passed on to children, and it is called extinct when its last native speaker dies. Language loss has been accelerating in recent decades, driven by a combination of social, economic, and political pressures that favor dominant languages over smaller ones.
>
> Globalization has played a central role. In many countries, economic opportunities are concentrated in cities where a few major languages dominate. Young people who hope to find good jobs or access higher education often feel pressure to abandon their ancestral languages in favor of these more widely spoken tongues. Media and technology compound the effect: films, social networks, and educational software are predominantly produced in a handful of global languages, reducing exposure to minority ones.
>
> Some scholars have questioned whether the loss of individual languages really matters, arguing that fewer languages might make communication easier. Most linguists, however, strongly disagree. Each language, they argue, contains unique knowledge about the world, including detailed vocabulary for local plants, animals, and ecosystems that may not exist in any other language. Languages also carry cultural memory, poetry, and ways of thinking that cannot be fully translated.
>
> Efforts to preserve endangered languages have grown. Some communities have established schools where children learn in their ancestral language; others have created dictionaries, apps, and recordings of elders speaking. While these efforts cannot save every language, they demonstrate that language loss is not inevitable. When communities, linguists, and governments work together, even severely endangered languages can sometimes be revived.
